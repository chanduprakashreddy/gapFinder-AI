# Cell Structure and Function

## Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells
Prokaryotic cells, such as bacteria, have no membrane-bound nucleus; their DNA lies in a region called the nucleoid. They are typically 1-5 micrometres across and have ribosomes of the 70S type. Eukaryotic cells, found in plants, animals, fungi and protists, are typically 10-100 micrometres across, have a true nucleus enclosed by a double membrane, membrane-bound organelles, and larger 80S ribosomes. Plant cells additionally have a cellulose cell wall, a large central vacuole and chloroplasts.

## Organelles
The nucleus stores DNA and controls gene expression. Mitochondria produce ATP through aerobic respiration and have their own circular DNA. The rough endoplasmic reticulum, studded with ribosomes, makes proteins for export, while the smooth endoplasmic reticulum makes lipids. The Golgi apparatus modifies, sorts and packages proteins into vesicles. Lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes that digest worn-out organelles and engulfed material.

## Transport across membranes
The plasma membrane is a fluid mosaic of phospholipids, proteins and cholesterol. Diffusion moves substances down their concentration gradient without energy. Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a partially permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential. Active transport moves substances against a concentration gradient and requires ATP and carrier proteins. An example is the sodium-potassium pump, which moves 3 sodium ions out and 2 potassium ions in for each ATP used.

# Photosynthesis

## Overview and the chloroplast
Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. The overall equation is 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy gives C6H12O6 + 6O2. It takes place in chloroplasts, which contain stacks of thylakoids called grana surrounded by a fluid called the stroma. Chlorophyll a is the main pigment; it absorbs mostly red and blue light and reflects green light.

## Light-dependent reactions
The light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes. Light excites electrons in photosystem II, and water is split by photolysis to replace them, releasing oxygen as a by-product. The electrons pass along an electron transport chain, which pumps protons into the thylakoid space. Protons flow back through ATP synthase to make ATP, a process called photophosphorylation. At photosystem I the electrons are re-energised and used to reduce NADP+ to NADPH.

## Calvin cycle
The Calvin cycle, also called the light-independent reactions, occurs in the stroma. The enzyme rubisco fixes carbon dioxide by attaching it to ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP), forming two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP). GP is reduced to triose phosphate using ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent reactions. Most triose phosphate is used to regenerate RuBP, and only one sixth leaves the cycle to make glucose and other organic molecules.

## Limiting factors
The rate of photosynthesis is limited by whichever factor is in shortest supply: light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration or temperature. At low light intensity, light is the limiting factor and the rate rises in proportion to intensity. Temperature affects the enzyme-controlled Calvin cycle; above about 40 degrees Celsius enzymes such as rubisco begin to denature and the rate falls.

# Cellular Respiration

## Aerobic respiration overview
Cellular respiration releases energy from glucose to make ATP. The overall equation for aerobic respiration is C6H12O6 + 6O2 gives 6CO2 + 6H2O plus about 30-32 ATP. It has four stages: glycolysis, the link reaction, the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation.

## Glycolysis and the link reaction
Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm and splits one glucose (6 carbons) into two pyruvate (3 carbons each), with a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 reduced NAD. It does not need oxygen. If oxygen is present, pyruvate enters the mitochondrial matrix, where the link reaction converts it to acetyl coenzyme A, releasing carbon dioxide and producing reduced NAD.

## Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation
The Krebs cycle takes place in the mitochondrial matrix. Each turn produces 1 ATP, 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD and 2 carbon dioxide per acetyl CoA. Oxidative phosphorylation occurs on the inner mitochondrial membrane, the cristae. Reduced NAD and FAD donate electrons to the electron transport chain, and oxygen is the final electron acceptor, forming water. This stage produces the majority of the ATP.

## Anaerobic respiration and fermentation
Without oxygen, glycolysis can continue only if NAD is regenerated. In animals, pyruvate is reduced to lactate in lactic acid fermentation. In yeast, pyruvate is converted to ethanol and carbon dioxide. Anaerobic respiration yields only 2 ATP per glucose.

# DNA and Replication

## Structure of DNA
DNA is a double helix of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands. Each nucleotide has a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group and one of four bases: adenine, thymine, cytosine or guanine. Adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds, and cytosine pairs with guanine through three. Chargaff's rules state that the amount of adenine equals thymine and the amount of cytosine equals guanine.

## Semi-conservative replication
Replication is semi-conservative: each new DNA molecule contains one original strand and one newly made strand. This was demonstrated by Meselson and Stahl using nitrogen-15 and nitrogen-14. DNA helicase unwinds and separates the strands by breaking hydrogen bonds. DNA polymerase adds nucleotides to the 3' end of the growing strand, so it can only build in the 5' to 3' direction.

## Leading and lagging strands
The leading strand is synthesised continuously towards the replication fork. The lagging strand is made discontinuously in short pieces called Okazaki fragments, each started from an RNA primer. DNA ligase later joins the fragments together.

# Mendelian Genetics

## Alleles and Mendel's laws
An allele is one version of a gene. An individual with two identical alleles is homozygous; with two different alleles, heterozygous. A dominant allele is expressed in the heterozygote; a recessive allele is expressed only when homozygous. Mendel's law of segregation states that the two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries one allele. The law of independent assortment states that alleles of different genes assort independently, provided the genes are on different chromosomes.

## Monohybrid and dihybrid crosses
A monohybrid cross between two heterozygotes (Aa x Aa) gives a genotype ratio of 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa and a phenotype ratio of 3 dominant : 1 recessive. A dihybrid cross between two double heterozygotes (AaBb x AaBb) gives a phenotype ratio of 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. A test cross with a homozygous recessive individual reveals whether an organism showing the dominant phenotype is homozygous or heterozygous.

## Codominance and sex linkage
In codominance both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote, as in the human ABO blood group where I-A and I-B are codominant and both are dominant to i. Sex-linked genes are carried on the X chromosome. Because males (XY) have only one X chromosome, recessive X-linked conditions such as haemophilia and red-green colour blindness are more common in males.
